Heart failure after MI: literature and data report

  • Question: What are the main risk factors for heart failure after acute MI, and which of them could we use to predict it in our patients?
  • Date: 2026-11-11

Part 1. What the literature says (Literature Scout)

I searched PubMed for studies on risk factors and prediction of heart failure (HF) after acute myocardial infarction (MI) and retrieved the abstracts below.

Overview

HF is the dominant post-MI complication today — more common than recurrent MI even in patients discharged with a normal ejection fraction (PMID 39874177). The strongest, most clinically usable predictors cluster into: (1) baseline clinical factors (age, prior HF, diabetes, renal dysfunction), (2) acute event severity (Killip class, in-hospital HF, hemodynamics), (3) biomarkers (natriuretic peptides, high-sensitivity troponin, hsCRP), and (4) imaging measures of infarct size and adverse remodeling (LV ejection fraction, left atrial volume, CMR tissue characterization).

Key papers

Butler et al. — Incident heart failure after acute MI (Eur Heart J 2025)

  • Design/Population: Retrospective contemporary cohort of 6,804 patients with a primary discharge diagnosis of MI at 28 U.S. hospitals (2015–2021), with landmark analysis anchored at 3 months post-discharge.
  • Main finding: Among patients without a HF history, 23.8% developed incident HF within 3 months of discharge (HF with EF<40%, 41–49%, and >50% in 42.2%, 26.7%, and 31.1%, respectively); risk of incident HF exceeded recurrent MI even in those with EF>50% and no HF at discharge, and incident HF predicted higher death and rehospitalization (PMID 39874177).

Asada et al. — "HF time-points" risk score in acute MI (J Clin Med 2024)

  • Design/Population: Multicenter registry of 1,331 acute MI patients undergoing PCI; HF risk scored at time-points (history of HF, elevated natriuretic peptides on admission, in-hospital HF events, elevated natriuretic peptides at ~31 days).
  • Main finding: Higher scores predicted cardiovascular death and HF rehospitalization in a stepwise fashion; a simple 3-time-point version (admission, in-hospital, short-term follow-up) was sufficient for estimating post-MI HF risk (PMID 38731029).

Neumann et al. — Cardiovascular biomarkers for population risk prediction (JAMA 2024)

  • Design/Population: Individual-level pooled analysis of 164,054 participants from 28 general-population cohorts across 12 countries, median follow-up 11.8 years.
  • Main finding: hs-cTnI, hs-cTnT, NT-proBNP, BNP, and hsCRP were each independently associated with incident HF; the incremental predictive value of adding biomarkers to established risk factors was most pronounced for HF and mortality (PMID 38739396).

Lv et al. — Plasma metabolomics and HF risk in coronary artery disease (Nat Commun 2024)

  • Design/Population: Nested case-control metabolomics study of 333 incident cardiovascular events and 333 matched controls in patients with coronary artery disease.
  • Main finding: Acylcarnitines were shared markers across events, while glycerophospholipid alterations were specific to HF; adding metabolites to clinical markers significantly improved HF risk prediction (PMID 38977723).

Wu et al. — Left atrial minimum volume index by cardiac MRI after AMI (Radiology 2025)

  • Design/Population: Prospective cohort of 1,191 AMI patients treated with PCI who underwent cardiac MRI; MACE included HF hospitalization.
  • Main finding: Larger LAVImin independently predicted MACE (HR 1.06, 95% CI 1.05–1.08) and added incremental value over traditional risk factors (C index 0.75 vs 0.69), outperforming LAVImax and LA strain (PMID 40956159).

Xie et al. — Machine learning with clinical + cardiac MRI in STEMI (Radiology 2026)

  • Design/Population: 1,066 STEMI patients (682 training, 384 external test set) with cardiac MRI within 7 days after PCI; MACE included rehospitalization for HF.
  • Main finding: A machine-learning model integrating clinical and CMR features achieved an integrated AUC of 0.91 in the external test set, versus 0.66 for GRACE and 0.62 for TIMI scores (PMID 41701025).

Gissler et al. — CMR evaluation of LV remodeling post-MI (JACC Cardiovasc Imaging 2024)

  • Design/Population: Review of adverse left ventricular remodeling (ALVR) and post-MI HF.
  • Main finding: ALVR and subsequent HF remain major causes of post-MI morbidity and mortality; overt inflammation drives myocardial fibrosis and ALVR, and CMR-derived structural/functional/tissue markers identify high-risk patients and serve as trial endpoints (PMID 38819335).

Butler et al. — EMPACT-MI: empagliflozin after acute MI (NEJM 2024)

  • Design/Population: Randomized, placebo-controlled trial of 6,522 patients hospitalized for acute MI who were at increased risk for HF (based on clinical, biomarker, and imaging risk markers).
  • Main finding: Empagliflozin did not significantly reduce first HF hospitalization or death overall (HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.76–1.06), though HF hospitalization was reduced (HR 0.77); the trial framework shows that a post-MI "at-risk for HF" population can be selected using clinical/biomarker/imaging features (PMID 38587237).

Gao et al. — Stress hyperglycemia ratio and outcomes (Ann Med 2023)

  • Design/Population: Prospective cohort of 1,179 patients with MI with non-obstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA), median follow-up 3.5 years; MACE included HF hospitalization.
  • Main finding: Higher stress hyperglycemia ratio (acute-to-chronic glycemia) independently predicted MACE (HR 2.30, 95% CI 1.21–4.38), improved on admission glucose alone especially in diabetics, and added discrimination to the TIMI score (PMID 36896774).

Gong et al. — Risk factors for in-hospital cardiac arrest in STEMI (JACC 2022)

  • Design/Population: Nationwide CCC-ACS registry of 40,670 STEMI patients (2014–2019).
  • Main finding: Age ≥75, female sex, prior diabetes, prior renal failure, heart rate >100 bpm, systolic BP <90 mmHg, and Killip class IV independently predicted in-hospital arrest; primary PCI, β-blockers, and ticagrelor were protective — illustrating modifiable acute-care predictors (PMID 36328690).

Risk factors and how to use them for prediction

Older age and sex

  • Older age and female sex are consistently associated with worse post-MI outcomes, including in-hospital arrest risk (PMID 36328690); age is a core variable in virtually every post-MI risk instrument (PMID 41701025).

Prior HF or HF during the index admission (Killip class)

  • History of HF and in-hospital HF events are the single strongest clinical anchors for future post-MI HF (PMID 38731029; PMID 39874177); Killip IV (cardiogenic shock) marks the highest-risk acute presentation (PMID 36328690).

Diabetes and stress hyperglycemia

  • Prior diabetes was an independent predictor of severe in-hospital events (PMID 36328690) and diabetes is highly prevalent (~48%) in post-MI cohorts with high incident HF (PMID 39874177); the admission stress hyperglycemia ratio adds prognostic information beyond glucose alone, including in non-diabetics (PMID 36896774).

Renal dysfunction

  • Prior renal failure/CKD is an independent predictor of severe post-MI complications (PMID 36328690) and is present in roughly a third of contemporary post-MI patients; it is strongly associated with MACE and mortality in atherosclerosis cohorts (PMID 39874177).

Ejection fraction, infarct size, and adverse remodeling

  • Reduced EF, HF with any EF spectrum (including preserved EF), and adverse LV remodeling drive incident HF (PMID 39874177); cardiac MRI measures — infarct tissue characterization, fibrosis, and left atrial minimum volume index — add incremental predictive value (PMID 40956159; PMID 38819335; PMID 41701025).

Natriuretic peptides, troponin, and inflammatory biomarkers

  • NT-proBNP/BNP, high-sensitivity troponin, and hsCRP each independently predict incident HF, with the greatest added predictive value seen for HF and mortality outcomes (PMID 38739396); elevated natriuretic peptides at admission and follow-up are the biomarker backbone of a validated post-MI HF risk score (PMID 38731029).

Emerging molecular and multimodality markers

  • Plasma metabolite panels (acylcarnitines, glycerophospholipids) improve HF risk prediction over clinical markers alone (PMID 38977723); machine-learning integration of clinical data and cardiac MRI outperforms GRACE and TIMI scores for long-term MACE including HF hospitalization (PMID 41701025).

What this means for our team

To predict HF in our post-MI patients, we should build from a simple clinical core — age, prior HF, Killip class/in-hospital HF, EF, renal function, and diabetes — then layer routinely available biomarkers (NT-proBNP/BNP, hs-troponin, hsCRP) and, where available, imaging (echo/CMR EF, left atrial volume, infarct size), ideally combined into a validated score or machine-learning model rather than relying on any single factor, since the evidence shows incremental value from each added domain (PMID 38739396; PMID 38731029; PMID 40956159; PMID 41701025).

2. Candidate feature sets

  • Set A — Admission core (literature-mapped): age, sex, prior_heart_failure, diabetes, prior_mi_count, systolic_bp_icu, ecg_rhythm_sinus_tachycardia, anterior_mi, lateral_mi. This maps directly to the literature's clinical core (age, sex, prior HF, diabetes, hemodynamics, infarct location).
  • Set B — Admission extended: Set A plus atrial_fibrillation_at_admission, white_blood_cell_count, serum_potassium, time_to_hospital. Adds a strong acute rhythm/congestion marker (AF p<0.001), a crude inflammation proxy, an electrolyte/metabolic variable, and treatment delay.
  • Set C — Admission + first 3 days: Set B plus pain_relapse_day_1, opioids_icu_day_1. Captures in-hospital course, but day 2/3 events may occur after HF has already developed.

Chosen final features: Set B. All 13 are known at admission and have ≤30% missing (max 21.12% for serum_potassium), with no leakage from later hospital days; it covers every literature domain our dataset can represent and adds variables judged worth testing despite weak unadjusted signals (time_to_hospital, white_blood_cell_count).

3. Data issues

  • Exclude serum_cpk (99.75% missing) and family_history_coronary_disease (95.73% missing): far too little data.
  • Exclude systolic_bp_emergency_team and diastolic_bp_emergency_team (62.70% missing, >30% threshold); use systolic_bp_icu (15.51%) instead.
  • Exclude cardiogenic_shock_at_admission and pulmonary_edema_at_admission: both are near-outcome severity markers, and cardiogenic_shock_at_admission shows HF 0.0% vs 23.8% — reflecting early death before HF can develop, which would bias a model.
  • Treat day 1–3 variables (pain_relapse_day_2/3, opioids_icu_day_1–3, nsaids_icu_day_1–3) with care: they are known only after admission, and the outcome is in-hospital HF, so they risk measuring consequences of the outcome.
  • Treat very small groups with care: ecg_av_block_2nd_degree_mobitz_2 has no variation; several history/ECG variables have cells of n=1–3 (e.g., history_ventricular_fibrillation, history_av_block_1st_degree).
  • Killip class, EF, imaging, and biomarker data are absent, so acute severity and infarct size are only approximated.

4. Predictive questions we can answer

  • Can admission clinical data predict in-hospital HF? Outcome heart_failure; predictors Set B; logistic regression with complete-case analysis and a missing-data sensitivity check.
  • Do ECG infarct-location markers (anterior_mi, lateral_mi) add predictive value beyond the clinical core (Set A)? Outcome heart_failure; logistic regression with likelihood-ratio test and AUC comparison.
  • Do acute rhythm/hemodynamic variables (atrial_fibrillation_at_admission, ecg_rhythm_sinus_tachycardia, systolic_bp_icu) improve discrimination over history variables alone? Outcome heart_failure; logistic regression, nested models.
  • Is the stage pattern of prior_heart_failure predictive beyond a simple yes/no? Outcome heart_failure; logistic regression treating prior_heart_failure as categorical (codes 0–4).
  • Does white_blood_cell_count (inflammation proxy) add value to the core model? Outcome heart_failure; logistic regression with reclassification metrics.

5. Questions we cannot answer

  • Do natriuretic peptides, high-sensitivity troponin, or hsCRP improve HF prediction? Not in our dataset (no biomarker variables).
  • Does renal dysfunction predict in-hospital HF? Not in our dataset (no creatinine, urea, or renal-history variables).
  • Does EF or any imaging measure (echo/CMR, LA volume, infarct size, remodeling) predict HF? Not in our dataset.
  • What predicts post-discharge incident HF? Not answerable — outcome is in-hospital HF only, with no follow-up after discharge.

Part 3. A fixed prediction model (no language model)

The Data Analyst chose the predictors; fixed code then fitted a logistic regression and scored it. The Data Analyst did not see any of these results.

  • Patients: 1,193 train (277 with heart failure) and 507 test (117 with heart failure).
  • Predictors used (13): age, sex, prior_heart_failure, diabetes, prior_mi_count, systolic_bp_icu, ecg_rhythm_sinus_tachycardia, anterior_mi, lateral_mi, atrial_fibrillation_at_admission, white_blood_cell_count, serum_potassium, time_to_hospital.
  • Model columns: 16 (17.3 training events per column).
  • 5-fold cross-validation in the training set: AUC 0.732 ± 0.019, PR-AUC 0.556, Brier 0.144.
  • Held-out test set: AUC 0.688, PR-AUC 0.433 (a model with no skill scores 0.231), Brier 0.158.
  • Training-set AUC (on the data the model learned from): 0.758.
